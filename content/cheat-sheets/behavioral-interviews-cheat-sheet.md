- **It is a graded round, not a warm-up.** The rubric is [[what-senior-means-as-a-level|the level]]:
  every story is evidence of scope — ambiguity owned, people led, a project driven to a result.
- **The rubric is written down at Amazon:** the 16 [[amazons-16-leadership-principles|Leadership
  Principles]] are the explicit scoring framework, and the best proxy for the private rubrics other
  companies use. Senior weight lands on mentorship, driving through ambiguity, and conflict —
  *Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit* (argue hard, then commit fully once decided).
- **Structure every answer as [[the-star-method|STAR]]:** **S**ituation (shortest — lead with the
  ambiguity), **T**ask (where "I" starts — what was *yours*), **A**ction (the bulk — your decisions,
  in the first person), **R**esult (quantified). The classic failure is a long Situation and a
  one-line Action; invert that budget.
- **Say "I," not "we."** The round scores *your* scope; a story told in "we" hides you. Attribute
  the team in one clause, then spend the answer on the decisions you made and the people you carried.
- **Quantify the result.** "It went well" is a wasted ending. Give the number — latency, cost, pages,
  incidents — or the nearest honest proxy. A number is also harder to inflate into a Staff-level claim.
- **Prepare stories, not principles.** Memorizing the 16 sounds hollow. Draft 6–8 real stories as
  STAR, tag each with the principles it evidences, and rehearse until conversational — one good story
  usually covers several principles at once.
- **Conflict is a strength beat.** Being overruled, told right, is a Disagree-and-Commit story: made
  the case with data, lost, committed fully, delivered. That is a senior signal, not a complaint.

The reach-for-it signal: any "tell me about a time you…" question. Run it along the STAR rail and
lead with how under-specified the situation was — ambiguity-ownership is the scope claim underneath
every prompt.
