Everything the vault holds for the leadership round, in the order that makes each note land — first
what the round is actually grading, then how to answer it, then the structure that keeps an answer
from wandering. It is a short path, and deliberately so: the behavioral round is not a large body of
knowledge to acquire, it is one claim to evidence and a handful of disciplines to rehearse. The vault
carries no reading order of its own — `prerequisites` is a graph and there are no lesson numbers — so
this is one path through that graph, and where a note disagrees with this page the note wins.

The one thing to notice before starting is that **the path opens outside the behavioral round
entirely.** Step 1 is [[what-senior-means-as-a-level]], because the behavioral round does not grade
whether you are pleasant or well-organised — it grades whether the senior scope claim is true of you,
told as stories in the first person. The rubric *is* the level. Read the frame first and the whole
round resolves into "evidence the ladder"; skip it and the disciplines below read as interview
etiquette rather than as the point.

## The order

| # | Read | Why here |
|---|------|----------|
| 1 | [[what-senior-means-as-a-level]] | The master lens, and not about the behavioral round at all: "senior" is a scope claim — ownership of ambiguity, leadership, end-to-end delivery — and the behavioral round is where that claim is proved directly rather than inferred from a solution. Read first because the note at step 2 records it as its prerequisite and every discipline below is an instruction for making one of those scope claims audible |
| 2 | [[the-behavioral-round-proves-the-ladder]] | What the round is and the three disciplines it grades: study the rubric (Amazon's Leadership Principles as the first-party proxy for every private ladder), say **"I," not "we,"** so the interviewer can find your scope, and **quantify the result** so impact is a fact rather than an adjective. It prerequisites step 1 and is the prerequisite for step 3 — it is the hub the other two notes hang off |
| 3 | [[the-star-method]] | The delivery structure: Situation, Task, Action, Result — a short setup that leads with the ambiguity, a Task in "I," an Action that is the bulk of the answer, a quantified Result mapped to a principle. Read last because it is the *shape* you pour the step-2 disciplines into, and it prerequisites step 2 for exactly that reason. Its own lesson is "prepare stories, not the letters" — the letters are scaffolding for the preparation, not a script for the delivery |

Steps 2 and 3 are best read as a pair in one sitting: step 2 is *what a good answer contains* and step 3
is *what shape you tell it in*, and neither is complete without the other. Step 1 can be read on its own
at any point — it is the lens the whole senior loop shares, not behavioral-specific — but it is placed
first here because the round makes no sense without it.

## Look this up rather than reading it

- [[amazons-16-leadership-principles]] — the rubric itself, one line each. This is a lookup you *scan*
  before a round, not prose you read through, which is why it is not a step in the path above. Open it
  while preparing your stories (below) and use it as the index you tag each story against — never as a
  list to memorise and recite, which is the one preparation both Lessons single out as the wrong one.

## Practice checkpoint

There is no coding Problem for this path, and — as with the [[reading-order-for-the-system-design-round|system-design
round]] — that is not a gap: the behavioral round is a **spoken performance from prepared material**, so
the practice is the preparation itself, done aloud. After step 3, take **six to eight real projects from
your own work** and draft each as Situation-Task-Action-Result: a short Situation that leads with how
under-specified the problem was, a Task in "I," an Action full of the decisions *you* made, and a Result
with a number in it. Tag each story with the [[amazons-16-leadership-principles|principles]] it evidences
— a good one usually covers several at once (a led migration where you were overruled and committed anyway
hits Ownership, Bias for Action, and Have Backbone in a single answer). Then rehearse them aloud until they
are conversational rather than recited. If you go quiet under a follow-up, that is the
[[what-senior-means-as-a-level|mission's]] named failure mode surfacing, and rehearsing the stories out
loud is exactly what defuses it.

## This is a rubric, not a runtime — what is company-specific and what transfers

The vault's other reading orders carry a caveat about which steps are .NET-specific. This one has no code
in it at all, so that caveat does not apply — but there is an analogous one worth stating plainly, because
the specificity here is by *company* rather than by language. The rubric this path studies is **Amazon's**,
and the reason it is used is that Amazon is the rare company that *publishes* the ladder its behavioral
round grades against. Most companies grade against an internal competency matrix they do not release.

What transfers, and what is Amazon's alone:

| The idea | How far it travels |
|---|---|
| The round grades a scope claim, not likeability | Universal. Every senior loop's behavioral round is checking ownership of ambiguity, leadership, and end-to-end delivery — the level, told as stories |
| Say "I," not "we"; quantify the result | Universal. These are how *any* interviewer finds your individual scope and writes down your impact, regardless of their rubric's wording |
| STAR as the answer structure | Near-universal. Situation-Task-Action-Result (and close variants like CAR / SOAR) is the standard behavioral-answer scaffold across the industry, not an Amazon invention |
| The 16 Leadership Principles, verbatim | **Amazon-specific.** The exact list, and the fact that interviewers score named principles aloud, is Amazon's. Elsewhere the LPs are a *proxy* for a private rubric — study their shape (mentorship, ambiguity, conflict carry the senior weight), not their wording |

The claim that survives any company: **prepare specific stories in which you owned ambiguity, made the call,
and can name the result in numbers, told in the first person** — that is what every behavioral rubric is
reaching for, whether or not it is written down.
